Carbon sequestration at country-level social costs of carbon

A separate valuation of the national flows, method as implemented, and the code of each step

This page describes a valuation of the national flows of the carbon sequestration accounts at the social cost of carbon of each country. It is a separate service of the tool, carbon_cscc, with its own workbook and report in outputs/carbon_cscc/, and it leaves the carbon sequestration accounts and their products unchanged. The numbers and tables that a run produces are in its report, which carries the same method text together with them.

1 Methods

1.1 Overview

The carbon sequestration accounts value the CO2 that ecosystems take out of the atmosphere at a global social cost of carbon, which is the damage that one more tonne of CO2 causes anywhere in the world. That price fits a service that benefits society as a whole. A second question is how much of that benefit stays in the country where the sequestration happens. The country-level social cost of carbon of Ricke, Drouet, Caldeira, & Tavoni (2018) answers it, since it is the damage that one more tonne of CO2 causes in one country alone. This valuation applies it to the same physical flows as the carbon sequestration accounts.

The valuation changes only the price. The actual flow and the missed flow of each country and year are those of the carbon sequestration accounts of the same version of the tool, described in their own documentation and report, and the steps that lead to them are not repeated here. The valuation is national, because the country-level social cost of carbon is one value for each country, so it has no regional tables and no maps. It is kept apart from the carbon sequestration accounts, which keep their global price, and it answers a different question. A country-level price also serves accounts of countries outside the European Union, where the same database holds a value for most countries of the world.

1.2 The country-level social cost of carbon

Ricke et al. (2018) estimate the social cost of carbon of each country as the present value of the change in its output that a pulse of CO2 emitted in 2020 causes up to 2200. They project output with the shared socio-economic pathways and warming with 26 climate models and 15 carbon cycle models, and they translate the warming of each country into a change of its growth rate with the empirical damage functions of Burke, Hsiang, & Miguel (2015), which relate economic growth to temperature. The global social cost of carbon is the sum of the values of all countries.

The database of the authors (Ricke, Drouet, Caldeira, & Tavoni, 2019) holds the values of several damage functions, scenarios and discount rates. A decision was made to use their reference case, which is the case that the article presents. It combines the short-run damage function of Burke et al. (2015), the pathway SSP2 with the forcing RCP 6.0, and growth-adjusted discounting with a pure rate of time preference of 2 percent and an elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption of 1.5. The database gives the median and the 16.7 and 83.3 percent quantiles of each value, which together cover two thirds of the estimates of the uncertainty analysis. The valuation uses the median for its central values and the two quantiles for its low and high values (see the stage fetch in the code annex).

The values can be negative. A country whose climate is cooler than the temperature at which output peaks in the damage function gains output from moderate warming, so one more tonne of CO2 raises its output, and Ricke et al. (2018) find this for many rich northern countries in 2020. Sequestration in those countries then has a negative value in this valuation. The authors add that trade, migration and other links between countries can bring costs to a country whose own value is negative, and that most values turn positive as warming continues (Ricke et al., 2018). Malta is not in the database, so it has no value.

Tol (2019) also estimates a social cost of carbon for most countries, with impact functions imputed from global estimates. That work treats the European Union as one unit, since climate policy is set at that level, so it gives no value for the Member States and cannot value their flows.

1.3 From US dollars to euros

The database expresses the values in US dollars of 2005 per tonne of CO2. The valuation moves them to the euros of 2023 in the same way as the carbon sequestration accounts move their global price, with the consumer price index of the United States from 2005 to 2023 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2023), a factor of 304.70/195.30, and the exchange rate of 2023, 1.0813 US dollars per euro (European Central Bank, 2023). The database does not state whether its dollars are at market exchange rates or at purchasing power parity. Open question. The conversion treats them as US dollars.

The price \(P_{c}\) of country \(c\) in 2023 euros is then a real value that stays the same in every year. As in the carbon sequestration accounts, the harmonised index of consumer prices \(H\) of the European Union (Eurostat, 2026) moves it to the euros of each year \(t\), and the deflator to the prices of 2010.

\[ P_{c,t} = P_{c}\, \frac{H_t}{H_{2023}}, \qquad V^{\text{current}}_{c,t} = F_{c,t}\, P_{c,t}, \qquad V^{\text{constant}}_{c,t} = V^{\text{current}}_{c,t}\, \frac{H_{2010}}{H_t} \tag{1}\]

Here \(F_{c,t}\) is the actual flow of the country in tonnes of CO2. The missed flow is valued in the same way. The total of the European Union adds up the values of its countries, each at its own price, and leaves out Malta (see the stage accounts in the code annex).

1.4 Products

Workbook. The workbook holds a sheet of prices, with the low, median and high value of each country in US dollars of 2005 and in euros of 2023, and sheets of the value of the actual flow and of the missed flow by country, in million euro at constant prices of 2010 and at current prices. Each sheet gives the low, central and high value of every reporting year, and its total row adds up the countries with a value. The physical flows are in the workbook of the carbon sequestration accounts.

1.5 Checks

The run stops if the national flows do not cover the 27 Member States, if the database has no global value, if a median lies outside its two quantiles, or if a value differs from the flow times its price and deflator.

Code annex

The service has one stage, which reads the national tables that esa run carbon writes to work/carbon/tables/, so the carbon sequestration accounts run first. esa run runs the services in the order pollination, wood, carbon and carbon_cscc. After a new run of the carbon sequestration accounts, esa run carbon_cscc --force values the new flows.

fetch

catalog.py, fetch.py · esa fetch carbon_cscc

fetch_cscc downloads the file cscc_db_v2.csv (27 MB) from the repository of the authors and keeps the rows of the reference case, one for each country and one for the world (WLD), with the 16.7 percent quantile, the median and the 83.3 percent quantile. It writes them to store/cscc/cscc_reference_case.parquet, records the date of retrieval, the address and the filter in extracts.json, and removes the download. The fetcher stops if the reference case does not give exactly one row for each country.

accounts

tables.py, accounts.py, workbook.py · esa run carbon_cscc -s accounts

Joins the values of the database to the Member States through their ISO3 codes in eu27_countries.csv, converts them to euros of 2023, and values the actual flow and the missed flow of carbon_value_country_year.parquet for each reporting year at the three quantiles. It writes carbon_sequestration_cscc_results.xlsx to outputs/carbon_cscc/tables/, runs the checks, and writes the facts and tables of the report.
